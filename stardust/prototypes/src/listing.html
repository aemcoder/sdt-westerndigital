Frequently Asked Questions

An internal hard drive (HDD) is a storage device installed inside a desktop computer or server that holds your operating system, applications, and files. It stores data magnetically on spinning platters and connects to the motherboard, usually via SATA. Internal HDDs offer large capacities at a low cost per gigabyte, making them ideal for bulk storage, backups, and media libraries.

SATA (Serial ATA) is the standard interface that connects internal hard drives to a computer's motherboard, transferring data at up to 6Gb/s. Nearly all desktop PCs have SATA ports, so a 3.5-inch SATA HDD is compatible with virtually any desktop. Just confirm you have an open SATA data port, a free power connector, and a 3.5-inch drive bay.

An internal HDD stores data on spinning magnetic platters, while an SSD uses flash memory with no moving parts. SSDs are much faster for booting, loading apps, and transferring files; HDDs are slower but deliver far more capacity per dollar. Many users pair an SSD for the operating system with a high-capacity HDD for storage and backups.

An internal hard drive provides the main long-term storage in a computer. It holds the operating system, installed programs, documents, photos, videos, and games, keeping that data saved even when the power is off. When you open a file or launch an app, the drive reads that data and delivers it to the system.

The right capacity depends on what you store. For everyday documents and web use, 1-2TB is usually plenty; for large photo and video libraries, gaming, or backups, 4-8TB or more is a better fit. Choosing more capacity than you need today leaves room to grow and helps you avoid running out of space later.

A NAS hard drive is an internal HDD built for the always-on, multi-drive environment of a network-attached storage system, with firmware and vibration tolerance tuned for 24/7 RAID operation. A standard desktop HDD can technically be installed, but it isn't rated for continuous use. For NAS, choose a purpose-built drive such as WD Red Plus or WD Red Pro.

Storage is typically used by the operating system, installed applications, system and temporary files, and personal data like photos, videos, and downloads. On Windows, you can review usage under Settings > System > Storage, and on macOS under Apple menu > System Settings > General > Storage. Removing unused apps, large media, and temporary files frees the most space.

CMR (Conventional Magnetic Recording) writes data tracks side by side, delivering consistent write performance that suits NAS, RAID, and write-heavy workloads. SMR (Shingled Magnetic Recording) overlaps tracks to pack in more capacity, which can slow sustained writes. CMR is recommended for RAID and NAS, while SMR fits archival or light, read-mostly use.

Desktop drives such as WD Blue are built for everyday single-user computing during normal working hours. Enterprise drives such as WD Gold and Ultrastar are engineered for 24/7 operation, higher workload ratings, longer warranties, and added reliability features like vibration resistance and error recovery. Enterprise drives cost more but are designed for servers, data centers, and continuous heavy use.

Surveillance NVR and DVR systems record continuously, so they need a drive rated for 24/7 write-intensive workloads rather than a standard desktop HDD. WD Purple and Purple Pro drives are purpose-built for surveillance, supporting always-on recording from multiple camera streams. Match the capacity to your number of cameras, recording resolution, and how long you need to retain footage.

RAID arrays need CMR drives rated for 24/7 operation with error-recovery and vibration-tolerance features. For NAS RAID, WD Red Plus or WD Red Pro are recommended; for enterprise or data-center RAID, WD Gold or Ultrastar drives are a better fit. Avoid standard SMR desktop drives in RAID, as they can slow or complicate array rebuilds.

SAS (Serial Attached SCSI) is an enterprise interface offering faster transfer speeds, dual data ports for redundancy, and support for large numbers of drives compared with SATA. It's used in servers, workstations, and data-center storage where reliability and scalability are priorities. Most home and desktop users don't need SAS; SATA is sufficient for typical PCs.

Partitioning divides one physical drive into separate logical volumes that the system treats as distinct drives. People partition to separate the operating system from data, run more than one operating system, or organize backups and files. It's optional for a single-purpose drive but useful when you want to keep system and personal data isolated.

The recognized standard for media sanitization is NIST Special Publication 800-88, which defines Clear, Purge, and Destroy methods. For a working HDD, a full overwrite or an ATA Secure Erase command removes data so it can't be recovered. For end-of-life drives that held sensitive data, physical destruction is the most thorough option.

WD Red is an entry-level NAS drive that uses SMR, spins at 5400 RPM class, and is best suited to light-duty or single-drive use. WD Red Pro is an enterprise-class CMR drive that spins at 7200 RPM, with a 550TB/year workload rating, up to a 2.5-million-hour MTBF, a 5-year warranty, and vibration sensors, making it the choice for large, multi-bay, 24/7 business NAS setups.

For a surveillance system in RAID, you want a drive that handles both continuous video writes and multi-bay array operation. WD Purple Pro is built for this, supporting 24/7 multi-stream recording plus the workload and reliability needed for RAID-based NVR setups and AI-enabled surveillance. Match total capacity to your camera count, resolution, and retention period.

A hard drive (HDD) is a storage device that saves your data, files, photos, applications, and the operating system, on spinning magnetic platters. It keeps that data even when the power is off, and reads or writes it whenever your computer or server needs it. HDDs offer large capacities at a low cost per gigabyte.

An HDD stores data on spinning magnetic platters, while an SSD uses flash memory with no moving parts. SSDs are much faster for booting and loading files, while HDDs deliver far more capacity per dollar. Many systems combine an SSD for speed with an HDD for high-capacity storage and backups.

Western Digital offers a full range of hard drives: internal desktop drives (WD Blue, WD Black), NAS drives (WD Red Plus, Red Pro), surveillance drives (WD Purple), and enterprise drives (WD Gold, Ultrastar), plus external and portable HDDs and data-center storage platforms.

An external hard drive sits outside your computer and connects over USB, adding storage without opening the machine, ideal for backups and portability. An internal hard drive installs inside a desktop computer or server and connects to the motherboard, usually via SATA, serving as the system's main built-in storage. The mechanism is similar; the difference is the enclosure and connection.

For long-term, offline archival storage, HDDs are generally preferred. They cost less per terabyte at high capacities and reliably retain data over years when stored properly. SSDs are faster but pricier per terabyte and can slowly lose charge if left unpowered for very long periods, so HDDs remain the workhorse for bulk archives.

A SAS (Serial Attached SCSI) hard drive uses an enterprise interface with dual ports for redundancy, faster speeds, and support for many drives. A business needs SAS when running servers, large RAID arrays, or data-center storage where reliability, failover, and scalability matter. For desktops and small NAS, the simpler SATA interface is usually sufficient.

CMR (Conventional Magnetic Recording) writes data tracks side by side for consistent write performance, making it the choice for NAS, RAID, and write-heavy workloads. SMR (Shingled Magnetic Recording) overlaps tracks to add capacity, but can slow under sustained writes. CMR suits active and RAID use; SMR fits archival or light, read-mostly storage.

Each is tuned for its workload. Desktop drives (WD Blue) handle everyday single-user computing; NAS drives (WD Red Plus/Pro) are built for 24/7 multi-drive RAID; surveillance drives (WD Purple/Purple Pro) handle continuous video recording from many cameras; and enterprise drives (WD Gold, Ultrastar) are designed for heavy, always-on data-center workloads with the highest reliability.

In these environments, hard drives provide cost-effective, high-capacity storage for backups, file servers, virtualization, surveillance, archives, and large-scale cloud and AI data. Their low cost per terabyte makes them ideal for storing data at scale. Enterprise drives and storage platforms deliver the 24/7 reliability and density these continuous workloads require.

Match the line to the workload: for NAS, choose WD Red Plus or Red Pro; for surveillance, WD Purple or Purple Pro; for data center and enterprise, WD Gold or Ultrastar; and for a high-performance workstation, WD Black, or WD Blue for general desktop use.
